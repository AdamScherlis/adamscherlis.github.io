Polyform tiling — validation ledger

This project reimplements the tiling-classification algorithms described on Joseph Myers's polyform tiling page: for every polyform of a given size, decide whether it tiles the plane, and how simply — by translations alone, by 180° rotation (Conway criterion), isohedrally, or only k-anisohedrally — or prove it cannot tile at all. Everything below is computed by a single lattice-generic engine that now runs on all eleven Laves tilings: polyominoes, polykites, polyhexes and polyiamonds are validated cell-for-cell against Myers's published tables, and the same code then classifies eight families with no published tiling classification — polycairos, polyrhombilles, polyflorets, polyprisms, polyaboloes, tetrakis polyaboloes, polydrafters and polypons.

The ledger currently stands at 1 936 200 free shapes and 532 633 one-sided ones — 2 468 833 classifications across the twelve families, all but sixty-two of them settled either as a tiling of some minimum isohedral number or as a proof that none exists — plus the hat and the turtle, whose refusal to settle is itself the answer. Among them are 3 726 anisohedral shapes (3 409 free, 317 one-sided): tiles that tile the plane, but never in a way that treats every copy alike. Forty of the free ones need four orbits and twenty-four need six.

Stage 3 · completeFull classification: isohedral, anisohedral, non-tilers n ≤ 10 validated

Candidate wallpaper subgroups of the base tiling's symmetry group are enumerated as (point subgroup, invariant sublattice, offset cocycle) triples modulo conjugacy; a k-isohedral tiling is a k-orbit exact cover of the quotient torus. Non-tiling is proved separately by possible-neighbour arc-consistency pruning, corona elimination, and a depth-d surround search (Myers's method). Every free polyomino through n = 10 is now classified, and every number below matches Myers's tables — all 4 655 10-ominoes in 204 s on 8 cores. Rows n = 11 and 12 (added 1 August) come from the new fused C+Python pipeline — enumeration, hole detection and translation tilings retired in C at ~10⁸ shapes/s, survivors finished by the Python engine — 17,073 shapes in 150 s and 63,600 in 13 minutes, matching Myers at every column including both anisohedral k-breakdowns. An entry like “35 487 + 1” means that many non-tilers proven, plus one shape still unresolved at kmax 4 (Myers lists it among his non-tilers; the totals agree).

Stage 5 · completeThe hat, rediscovered aperiodicity evidence

Classifying all 873 free 8-kites left exactly one shape unresolved — and it is the hat, the aperiodic monotile of Smith, Myers, Kaplan and Goodman-Strauss (2023). The engine wasn't told about it; it simply survived every classification the engine knows, which is precisely the computational signature of aperiodicity Myers describes:

  • It tiles locally: surrounds (coronas) exist to depth 6 and counting — a certified Heesch lower bound of 6, with no failure in sight (the true value is ∞, since it tiles the plane).
  • But never periodically: exhaustive search over all candidate wallpaper subgroups finds no tiling with isohedral number k for any k ≤ 14 (originally k ≤ 7 by exact-k backtracking; the SAT exclusion engine later swept k ≤ 14 in thirty seconds — positive controls: the engine finds the 4-anisohedral nonomino and 3-anisohedral kites).

A shape that surrounds indefinitely but admits no k-isohedral tiling at any searched k is either aperiodic or extraordinarily anisohedral — Myers's programs would "run for ever" on it; ours reports it unresolved and hands over exactly this evidence. The same signature has since singled out the turtle among the 10-kites (Stage 4) — the engine has now rediscovered both of the known aperiodic polykites blind.

Resolved (30 July, afternoon): the star does not tile. As a free 20-iamond it tiles by mere translation, but aligned to the rhombille grid it is a proven non-tiler with Heesch number exactly 3 — and, as a posthumous consistency check, a SAT version of the k-orbit search has since pushed its periodic exclusion from k ≤ 15 (4.3 CPU-hours of backtracking) to k ≤ 18 in seven minutes, with every group refutation through k = 15 DRAT-certified. Isohedral number 10 — the project record, on nine triangles. The n = 9 row's lone holdout survived every sweep: no tiling with k ≤ 4, and unlike almost every other unresolved shape in the project it also refused to be proven a non-tiler — its surrounds run to depth 6 and show no sign of stopping. That combination is the star's signature, and for a few hours it was the project's new flagship mystery. It is instead the highest-k tile here: the SAT orbit search returns MIN-K = 10, with the cover cross-checked as an exact torus partition (40 tiles per fundamental domain in ten orbits of 36 cells each) and independently re-verified by expanding the 40 representatives back onto the torus — all 360 cells covered exactly once, no duplicates. The chevron and a minimal tiling of it, coloured by orbit — ten classes of tile. The previous record in this project was 8 (two 10-hexes and a 13-iamond, found the same night); before that, 6. Stage 15 · new resultsSpectre mode: reflections banned eleven families

Chiral edge notches (as on the Spectre) enforce grid alignment and orientation, so classification under direct isometries only — rotations and translations, shapes one-sided — is the version realizable by an honest unmarked notched tile, with no grid caveat. Eleven of the project's twelve families now carry explicit one-sided classifications: the eight tables below (polyominoes, polyhexes, polyiamonds, polycairos, polykites, polyrhombilles, polyaboloes, polyprisms) plus the one-sided tables kept with their families in Stages 12–14 (tetrakis polyaboloes, polydrafters, polypons). The twelfth family needs no separate table at all: the floret grid is chiral (p6, no reflections to ban), so polyflorets are intrinsically one-sided and their main table in Stage 9 already is their Spectre classification. Where one-sided enumerations are published the counts validate externally: polyominoes match A000988, polyhexes A006535, and polyiamonds A006534, at every computed term.

A uniform result with a single exception: all eleven 4-anisohedral polycairos (n = 7, 9, 11) are Spectre-mode non-tilers in both chiralities — every minimal 4-orbit tiling in the Cairo family depends on reflections — and the pattern is project-wide. The census of free 4-anisohedral shapes now stands at forty across six families — four polyominoes, eight polyhexes, seven polyiamonds, eleven polycairos, one enneabolo and nine tetrakis polyaboloes (the new 1 August rows more than doubled it) — of which nineteen have been put through Spectre mode: the twelve at sizes their family's one-sided sweep reaches, plus the seven 11-cairos settled by targeted probes. Stage 16 · new resultsWang decorations: bump/dent matching rules third audit closed · v3 oracle-verified

The engine supports edge decorations: every boundary edge of a shape carries a label — 0 is neutral (matches itself), labels 1 and 2 are bump/dent partners that match only each other, exactly the Wang-tile mechanism behind the Spectre’s notches. The question this machinery answers: what do decorations do to a shape’s isohedral number? It took three audits to earn a trustworthy answer.

How this section was wrong twice. The first release showed a decorated L-tromino “4-anisohedral” tiling with overlapping bump discs; a reader flagged it, and the overlaps turned out to be bump-meets-bump contacts in a phantom tiling — the validator’s translate window missed touching pairs under skewed sublattice bases (too permissive). The v2 rerun fixed that and was promptly caught the other way: a reader observed that the new “4-orbit” figure looked transitive, and brute force agreed — orbit count 1. Placements had been identified by cell footprint, which conflates the mirror aspects of a symmetric shape; the decoder could pick the label-wrong aspect and reject genuine covers, silently inflating every k (too strict). The v3 engine keys placements by label class, decodes orbits from seeds by the group action, keeps the independent patch-level post-check on every success — and every positive below has been re-verified by a brute-force orbit-count oracle that computes the found tiling’s full decorated symmetry group from scratch: 160 of 160 k = 2 verdicts exact, zero violations.

The parity lemma (unchanged, and doing most of the work): with the identity mirror action, a decoration whose bump-count differs from its dent-count for any bump/dent pair tiles at no k whatsoever — bumps and dents must pair up across shared edges, and every tile carries the same labels. On an eight-edge shape this dismisses 5 454 of 6 561 three-label decorations before any search runs.

The v3 truth is more rigid than either wrong version. All eleven Laves unit prototiles — the hexagon included, contrary to v2’s prized “k = 3 hexagon” — are decoration-rigid: every 3-label bump/dent decoration either leaves the prototile isohedral or kills every tiling within the search bound; nothing in between. (The rhombus keeps its clean geometric rule: it tiles iff each edge pair meeting at a wide corner is self-compatible.) The polyominoes manage exactly one notch of the ladder: the domino is rigid too ({1}), while the L-tromino, I-tromino and O-tetromino each have a genuine population of 2-anisohedral decorations (80, 48 and 32 of 6 561) — a bump/dent rule can double the isohedral number of a translation-friendly shape, and, as far as every sound sweep so far can see, no 3-label decoration of any shape tested exceeds k = 2. Richer alphabets don’t help: 5-label sweeps (two independent bump/dent pairs) of the hexagon and domino produce more isohedral decorations and nothing anisohedral at all. The open question flipped: earlier drafts asked whether the hexagon’s “ceiling of 3” was real; the real question is whether any edge-matching decoration of any polyform can beat 2 — and if one can, how large it has to be.

Arrows: reflect a bump into a dent, and the ceiling breaks

Every sweep above uses the identity mirror: a reflected bump is still a bump. That single modelling choice is what powers the parity lemma — and, it turns out, what caps the isohedral numbers. Give the notches an asymmetric profile instead, so that a reflected bump reads as a dent (mirror : 1 ↔ 2, the natural physical model of an arrowhead notch), and the parity argument evaporates: a reflected tile carries swapped bump and dent counts, so unbalanced decorations can balance their census by mixing chiralities. The prediction was logged before running the sweep — and the sweep breaks the ceiling wide open.

Of the 729 three-label arrow decorations of a single regular hexagon: 195 tile isohedrally, 102 are 2-anisohedral, 12 are 4-anisohedral, and 24 are 5-anisohedral — against a hard ceiling of k = 1 for the same hexagon under identity mirror. Every one of the 138 anisohedral verdicts was re-derived by the brute-force orbit-count oracle, with zero violations. (Curiously k=3 is absent, and the domino — 729 arrow decorations — stays entirely isohedral, all 729 of them: the mechanism is not simply “more freedom everywhere”.)

The complete taxonomy of edge markings

The arrow rule is not an ad-hoc variant: it is one of exactly four possibilities, and working out why says what the whole decorated-tiling game consists of. A marking on a shared edge is recorded by each adjacent tile in its own frame — boundary traversal direction, interior on the left. Neighbouring frames differ by ρ, the half-turn about the edge midpoint, so “these two tiles fit” means exactly your record is mine turned by ρ; and placing a tile by a reflection acts on records by σ⊥, reflection across the edge's perpendicular bisector. So the symmetry subgroup of the marking's own profile, inside {1, ρ, σ∥, σ⊥}, determines the matching rule completely:

The S-tab is the ρ-symmetric row and the one that feels least like the others: because its profile is carried onto itself by the half-turn, an S-tab fits another S-tab — the same tile rotated 180° — rather than a complementary partner. Its mirror image is a Z-tab, which it refuses. So its pairing is the identity, a combination no sweep in this project had ever used. Of the hexagon's 729 three-label S-tab decorations, 615 tile isohedrally, 12 are 2-anisohedral, and 102 admit no tiling at all — permissive where the arrow rule is demanding, and capped far below the arrow rule's k = 5. Self-matching markings, it turns out, mostly get out of their own way.

A sweep-wide economy. Every sweep above was, until this morning, re-searching decorations it had already answered: two decorations related by a symmetry of the shape, or by a global relabelling that commutes with the pairing and mirror maps, pose the same problem and must have the same minimum k. Quotienting by both groups before searching costs a few seconds of bookkeeping and cuts the work by 14× on the hexagon and 38× on the four-label scheme — verified by reproducing the arrow-hexagon spectrum exactly, decoration for decoration. Sweeps that had been priced at four hours now run in fifteen minutes, which is what made the four-label sweeps affordable at all.

The ceiling belongs to the marking type; the alphabet only sets abundance. Three further sweeps settle how these rules relate. The four-label fully asymmetric tab — the richest type in the taxonomy — reaches only k = 2 on the hexagon ({1: 989, 2: 108}, and 14 528 of its 15,625 decorations admit no tiling at all): richness buys non-tilers, not orbits. A five-label arrow alphabet (two independent arrow pairs) keeps the arrow ceiling exactly — still k = 5, nothing at 6 — but finds four times as many high-k decorations (48 at k = 4 and 48 at k = 5, against 12 and 24 with one pair). And pushing the three-label arrow hexagon to kmax 8 leaves its spectrum untouched: no decoration of a hexagon reaches 6, 7 or 8 orbits, and the 396 that fail to tile fail at every k ≤ 8. So the profile's symmetry class fixes how high a shape can be driven, while extra label pairs of the same type only multiply the solutions at that height.

The shape matters as much as the marking, though. The S-tab, capped at k = 2 on the hexagon, reaches k = 3 on the L-tromino (3 159 isohedral, 30 at k = 2, 8 at k = 3, 3 364 with no tiling at k ≤ 4) — eight decorations that force three orbits on a shape which, undecorated, tiles by translation.

The same hexagon, a different grid, the same five orbits. The retraction above named the experiment that would settle things: find a second shape with both properties. Searching all eleven grids for shapes with six or more edges and a large symmetry group turned up seventeen candidates, and one is irresistible — six triangles merged into a regular hexagon. It is geometrically the same shape as the hex prototile, with the same six edges and the same twelve symmetries, but it lives on the finer triangular grid, where it can sit at offsets the hexagonal grid forbids. Sampled in the density band, it reaches k = 5 — carrying the very same labels, (0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 2), as the hexagon's record decoration. The companion test cuts the other way. The 2 × 2 square has eight edges and eight symmetries — the first shape tested to clear both thresholds without being a hexagon — and it is edge-transitive besides. Every one of 150 sampled decorations still tiles isohedrally. So the two thresholds are necessary and manifestly not sufficient, and the tidy story that survives is a short one: on these eleven grids the only shape anyone has pushed past two orbits is the regular hexagon, in either of its realizations. Whether that is because twelve symmetries and six edges is genuinely the sweet spot, or merely because these grids cannot build anything else with both, is still open — shapes with twelve edges and twelve symmetries exist here, and are being swept now.

And an honest retraction. An earlier version of this section claimed the driver was edge count alone, on the grounds that the trihex has a larger symmetry group than the dihex yet performs worse. That comparison is confounded — the trihex has more edges as well as more symmetry — and the table above contains its own refutation: the square domino has exactly the hexagon's six edges and a quarter of its symmetry, and not one of its 729 arrow decorations forces more than a single orbit. At fixed edge count, symmetry matters enormously. Conversely the square prototile has eight symmetries and only four edges, and also forces nothing. What the data actually supports is narrower and duller: the hexagon is the only shape tested with both six or more edges and a symmetry group of order eight or more, and both deficiencies — too few edges, too little symmetry — are individually fatal. With a single specimen in that corner, the two effects cannot be separated. Finding a second shape with both properties, on any grid, is the experiment that would settle it.

Mixing types on one tile is expressible too — the engine's pairing and mirror are arbitrary label maps, so each marking type simply occupies its own range of labels, and a single shape can carry a jigsaw tab on one edge, an arrow on the next and an S-tab on a third. That sweep is the obvious next one, along with two-cell shapes under the arrow rule, where the hexagon's k = 5 record might finally be beaten.

The domino row is the counterpoint worth keeping in view: under the arrow rule every single one of its 729 decorations tiles isohedrally — the extra freedom that lets the hexagon climb to five orbits lets the domino escape every constraint. Whether the hexagon's k = 5 is a new ceiling or just the first rung is open; the sweeps of larger shapes are queued.

A methodological coda: v1’s bug could only accept too much, v2’s could only reject too much, and each produced a plausible-looking mathematical narrative — spectra with “gaps”, a special hexagon — that dissolved under an independent check the search code had no hand in. The surviving facts are duller and firmer: rigidity everywhere, one honest doubling, and a pre-filter lemma that does more than the search does.

Stage 18 · new resultsTwo algorithms, measured against each other 1 762 shapes, zero disagreements

This project's engine and Myers's differ at the root. He runs a class-based backtracking search: start with one tile, branch over every way a neighbour can touch an unsurrounded tile, let it join a known class or open a new one, propagate the tiles the resulting symmetries force, and stop when every class representative is surrounded — the symmetry group is never enumerated, it is discovered. We enumerate candidate wallpaper groups up front and solve an exact cover on each quotient torus. Agreement between the two has been this page's main external check, so it was worth asking the sharper question: which is actually faster, and why? A faithful reimplementation of his method now lives beside ours (myers_isohedral.py), verdict-for-verdict identical on all 1,762 free polyominoes at n = 7, 8, 9 — zero disagreements, with every structure it accepts independently developed into a patch and re-verified as an exact torus partition.

What our side keeps is the shape of the answer rather than the speed: “no tiling with k ≤ K” is a finite enumeration for us — finitely many groups, each a finite exact cover — which is exactly why it could be handed to a SAT solver and come back with a DRAT certificate, while a backtracking refutation is an unreplayable trace. The group-theoretic formulation also carries to all eleven Laves grids with no per-family reasoning, and decorations ride along on the orbits for free. The natural conclusion is a hybrid rather than a winner: route by difficulty, exact cover first and the class search as tail-breaker, and import two of his devices we cannot currently exploit — possible-neighbour bitmaps used inside the search, and forced-tile propagation read as unit propagation over class-relative surround variables.

How the engine works

One engine, eleven grids. Every base tiling is described combinatorially: cells are integer points, the tiling's symmetries act as affine integer maps, and a backend only supplies the codes, matrices and cell polygons (adjacency is derived and the group axioms are machine-checked — no floating point anywhere). A periodic tiling by a shape P with symmetry group G ⊆ W collapses to a finite statement: the images of P under G's finitely many coset representatives must exactly partition the torus of cells modulo G's translation sublattice. So tiling questions become exact-cover problems over enumerated subgroups — one orbit for isohedral, k orbits for k-isohedral — and non-tiling is proved separately by Myers's neighbour-pruning, corona elimination and depth-d surround search. This differs from Myers's own class-based backtracking implementation, which makes the replication a genuinely independent check of his tables.

The SAT pivot. Both hard search regimes turned out to be CDCL-solver territory. Surround existence: placements become Boolean variables; each grid cell gets an at-most-one constraint over the placements covering it; corona membership is forced level by level, so “a depth-d surround exists” is one satisfiability query over a placement universe provably closed under everything the recursive search could reach. The star's depth-3 question — hours of inconclusive backtracking — became a 0.2-second SAT call, and its depth-4 refutation a 7-second UNSAT. Periodic exclusion: per candidate group, orbit masks become variables, every torus cell gets an exactly-one constraint, and a cardinality bound at the group's admissible k makes one UNSAT query exclude every k at once; the star's k-exclusion moved from 4.3 CPU-hours (k ≤ 15) to seven minutes (k ≤ 18). Positive answers decode to concrete tilings and are re-verified independently; negative answers emit DRAT proof logs checked by drat-trim, so refutations no longer rest on trusting any search code — ours or the solver's.

The audit trail. This page's numbers have survived four soundness episodes, two of them caught by a reader of the figures, and it seems more honest to display that history than to bury it. (1) An instrumentation ambiguity let node-budget cap-hits be read as found surrounds — caught in an internal audit; every claim was re-derived and the reporting disambiguated. (2) The decorated-tiling validator checked label compatibility over a fixed window of lattice translates in Hermite coordinates; skewed bases let genuinely touching tiles escape the window, and phantom decorated tilings slipped through — caught by a reader noticing overlapping bump discs, which turned out to be bump-meets-bump contacts no valid tiling could contain. Fixed with a Gauss-reduced basis, a span-derived window, and an independent patch-level post-check on every decorated success. (3) Decorated placements were identified by their cell footprint, which conflates mirror aspects whose labels differ; the decoder could pick the wrong aspect and reject genuine covers, inflating decorated isohedral numbers — caught by a reader observing that a published “4-orbit” tiling looked transitive (it was; brute force confirms orbit count 1). The v3 engine keys placements by label class and verifies every positive against a brute-force orbit-count oracle. (4) The SAT cross-checks exposed that a wrap-around placement whose torus footprint is merely setwise-stabilized can enter an “orbit” whose plane expansion double-covers — a false-positive-only risk; the systematic re-audit came back clean (all 37 stored anisohedral examples re-derived exactly). The pattern worth stating: every bug was found because some independent artifact — a figure, a cross-check, a second engine — contradicted the search, which is the strongest argument this project knows for computing everything twice.
