How polyforms tile the plane

Take a shape made by gluing together n cells of a regular grid — a polyomino, a polyhex, a polykite. Does it tile the plane? If it does, how simple can the tiling be? This project answers both questions exactly, for every shape up to a given size, on fifteen different grids: 115 025 990 shapes so far, each one either given its simplest tiling or proved not to tile at all.

The question

A tiling by copies of one shape is isohedral if its symmetries can carry any tile onto any other: every copy plays the same role. Many shapes tile only in less regular ways. A tiling is k-isohedral if its tiles fall into k classes (orbits) under its symmetry group, and a shape's isohedral number is the smallest k for which it has a k-isohedral tiling. Shapes with isohedral number above one are called anisohedral; the first was found by Heesch in 1935, answering part of Hilbert's eighteenth problem.

Following the hierarchy used by Joseph Myers, each shape goes into the first class that applies:

hole
the shape encloses a hole, so it cannot tile.
translation
tiles using translated copies only.
half-turn
tiles using translations and 180° rotations (Conway's criterion).
isohedral
has some other tiling with a single orbit.
k-anisohedral
tiles, but needs at least k ≥ 2 orbits.
non-tiler
proved not to tile: at some finite distance no arrangement of copies can surround it.

A shape that fits none of these — it can be surrounded as far as anyone looks, yet has no periodic tiling with any number of orbits tried — is reported as unresolved. That is the computational signature of an aperiodic monotile, and the only shapes that end up there are the known ones (below).

One convention throughout: tilings are aligned to the grid. For polyhexes this is no restriction at all, since regular hexagons fit together in only one way. For polyominoes and polyiamonds it is the classical convention, and the results agree with the published tables at every size compared. On the other grids it is a real restriction: a polyrhombille is also a polyiamond and may tile as one in ways that ignore the rhombille grid. Isohedral numbers quoted for those families are for grid-aligned tilings, and can be higher than the shape's isohedral number as a bare polygon.

Main results

Independent agreement with the published tables

Myers tabulates these classes for polyominoes, polyhexes, polyiamonds and polykites. The method here is different from his — periodic tilings are found by exact cover over an explicit list of candidate symmetry groups, rather than by backtracking over tile classes — and it reproduces his counts in every column of every row both have computed: polyominoes to n = 15, polyhexes to 13, polyiamonds to 18 and polykites to 15, 18 890 736 shapes in all, including the breakdown of anisohedral shapes by isohedral number. The comparison is run automatically against a copy of his tables every time this site is built.

Eleven families classified for the first time

The same engine runs on any periodic grid described by integer data, so it extends to the remaining Laves tilings — polycairos, polyrhombilles, polyflorets, polyprisms, polyaboloes, tetrakis polyaboloes, polydrafters and polypons — and to three Archimedean tilings whose cells are not all the same shape. To our knowledge none of these had published tiling classifications; together they account for 61 886 176 of the shapes here. The family tables give every row.

Tiles that need many orbits

Most shapes that tile do so isohedrally. Of the free shapes here, 16 438 are anisohedral, and the counts fall off fast with k — then stop falling at six, and skip seven entirely.

Seventy-two shapes need six or more orbits when tilings must follow the shape's own grid (52 distinct polygons, since some appear on more than one grid). For the polyhexes among them that is the whole truth: the largest is an 11-hex needing nine orbits, which with two eight-orbit 10-hexes and an eight-orbit 13-iamond confirms entries in Myers's tables.

On the other grids the convention matters, and it can be tested by cutting the same polygon into smaller cells and classifying it again. Of 342 anisohedral shapes tested this way, 168 keep their isohedral number — every polykite among them — while 164 turn out to tile isohedrally once freed from their grid. The latter include a nine-cell tetrakis shape that needs ten orbits on its own grid, the largest number in the dataset, and tiles by half-turns off it. Of 46 hard non-tilers tested, 31 tile after all. Hard shapes has the details, and every shape drawn with its fate.

Aperiodic monotiles, found without being told

The classifier knows nothing about the hat. Among the 873 8-kites it leaves exactly one shape unresolved, and it is the hat of Smith, Myers, Kaplan and Goodman-Strauss; among the 10 011 10-kites the single shape left is the turtle. The hat then turns up a third time, as the only unresolved 16-drafter, on a grid where each kite is two cells. No other shape in any family ends unresolved.

A separate search follows the hat off its grid, to the equilateral polygons among which the Spectre was found. Of 4 617 such polygons, each tried under three different rules for which copies may be used, it again singles out the hat's own polygon and nothing else.

Proofs that shapes do not tile

Non-tiling is proved by showing that a shape cannot be surrounded to some depth. The hardest cases go to a SAT solver, and the resulting refutations can be checked by an independent proof checker. One ten-rhombus star surrounds itself three times and provably not a fourth; its refutation is certified. Heesch numbers are tabulated for every non-tiler that needed such a proof.

Edge markings

Marking the edges of a tile with matching rules — bumps and dents, arrows — changes what it can do. A regular hexagon with arrow-marked edges can be forced to need five orbits. Of 142 782 marked tiles for which a search found no periodic tiling, every one is now proved to have no tiling of any kind, periodic or not. Edge markings has the survey.
