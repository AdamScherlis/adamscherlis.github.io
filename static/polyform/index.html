This project reimplements the tiling-classification algorithms described on Joseph Myers's polyform tiling page: for every polyform of a given size, decide whether it tiles the plane, and how simply — by translations alone, by 180° rotation (Conway criterion), isohedrally, or only k-anisohedrally — or prove it cannot tile at all. Everything below is computed by a single lattice-generic engine that now runs on all eleven Laves tilings and three Archimedean ones: polyominoes, polykites, polyhexes and polyiamonds are validated cell-for-cell against Myers's published tables, and the same code then classifies eight Laves families with no published tiling classification — polycairos, polyrhombilles, polyflorets, polyprisms, polyaboloes, tetrakis polyaboloes, polydrafters and polypons — and then three substrates whose cells are not all the same shape: the trihexagonal, truncated square and truncated hexagonal tilings (Part VII), which no polyform classification had touched before.

The ledger currently stands at 49 274 865 free shapes and 905 386 one-sided ones — 50 180 251 classifications across the fifteen families, all of them settled either as a tiling of some minimum isohedral number or as a proof that none exists — plus the hat and the turtle, whose refusal to settle is itself the answer. Among them are 13 548 anisohedral shapes (12 453 free, 1 095 one-sided): tiles that tile the plane, but never in a way that treats every copy alike. Of the free ones, 11 359 need two orbits and 901 need three; then it thins out fast — 138 need four, thirteen need five, thirty-nine need six, three need eight, one needs nine and one needs ten.

Part I · replicationPolyominoes: isohedral, anisohedral, non-tilers n ≤ 12 validated

Candidate wallpaper subgroups of the base tiling's symmetry group are enumerated as (point subgroup, invariant sublattice, offset cocycle) triples modulo conjugacy; a k-isohedral tiling is a k-orbit exact cover of the quotient torus. Non-tiling is proved separately by possible-neighbour arc-consistency pruning, corona elimination, and a depth-d surround search (Myers's method). Every free polyomino through n = 10 is now classified, and every number below matches Myers's tables — all 4 655 10-ominoes in 204 s on 8 cores. Rows n = 11 and 12 (added 1 August) come from the new fused C+Python pipeline — enumeration, hole detection and translation tilings retired in C at ~10⁸ shapes/s, survivors finished by the Python engine — 17 073 shapes in 150 s and 63 600 in 13 minutes, matching Myers at every column including both anisohedral k-breakdowns. An entry like “35 487 + 1” means that many non-tilers proven, plus one shape still unresolved at kmax 4 (Myers lists it among his non-tilers; the totals agree).

The n = 10 row (added 31 July) is a triple event. First, agreement with Myers holds at every column — 702 holey shapes, 9 294 non-tilers, and not one 10-kite that tiles by translation, 180° or isohedrally. Second, of Myers's fifteen anisohedral entries our classifier leaves three unresolved at kmax 4, and exact k = 5 searches plus k = 6 covers settle two of them as the family's first 6-anisohedral polykites — kites join the rhombilles, florets and polypons in the isohedral-number-6 club — which the 13-rhombille probes then grew to twenty-three shapes across four Laves families. n = 11 (added 1 August) is the family at its most austere, and the first new row here to come back fully resolved with no probes needed. The iamonds are gentler but end on a cliffhanger. Exactly one heptiamond fails to tile (the V), then every one of the 66 octiamonds tiles the plane — the largest non-tiler-free size in any family here. Four new rows (1 August) take the iamonds to n = 14, and the family turns out to be the steadiest performer on the page: 39 921 shapes classified at n = 11–14, three of the four rows matching Myers at every column including both k-breakdowns, and only five shapes left open in total. Polyprisms (prismatic pentagonal, [3.3.3.4.4] Laves, cmm; counts match the known 1, 3, 5, 20, 56, 225) are the friendliest family yet — the single pentagon tiles by 180°, the first non-tiler appears only at n = 4, and n = 7 brings the first 3-anisohedral polyprism. At n = 9 the anisohedral count triples (42, including a second 3-anisohedral shape) and the family stays fully resolved — no unresolved polyprism exists through n = 10, and none ever has: every polyprism the engine has enumerated was settled by the batch sweep alone, with no deep probe ever needed.

The n = 10 row (added 1 August) is the family's largest and by some distance the cheapest big row on the page: all 55 231 free 10-prisms classified in 29 seconds, four times the shapes of the n = 9 row for three seconds more runtime. Part II · new familiesThe shape of two and a half million answers every family, one picture

Twelve families, 2.5 million classifications, and one question worth asking of all of them at once: what fraction of shapes actually tile the plane, and how does that change with size? Twelve series is far past what any honest categorical palette can carry, so each family gets its own panel; within a panel the only colour is odd versus even size, because that turns out to be the story.

Percentage of free shapes of each size that tile the plane (rows with fewer than 20 shapes omitted). The four classical families decline smoothly; the Laves families built from several cells per translation unit oscillate violently, and the oscillation is not noise.

The top row behaves the way intuition says it should. Polyominoes, polyhexes and polyiamonds decline smoothly — bigger shapes are likelier to be awkward, and the curve slides from “almost everything tiles” toward “almost nothing does.” The exotic grids do something else entirely: polypons swing between 1% and 36%, tetrakis polyaboloes between 0% and 71%, size after size, in a sawtooth that persists to the largest rows computed.

The sawtooth is arithmetic, not luck. Each of these grids has c cells per translation unit — three rhombi to a hexagon, four quarter-squares to a square, six pons to a triangle — and a tiling has to cover a quotient torus of exactly c·m cells using whole copies of an n-cell shape. Counting the copies gives the constraint the engine checks before it enumerates a single group: c must divide n·T, where T is a sum of k terms of the form q/s — q the order of the tiling group's point group, s the order of an orbit's stabiliser. The exact arithmetic matters less than its shape: T ranges over a sparse set of integers, so when n shares no factor with c whole classes of candidate group vanish before any search begins, and when n is a multiple of c they all survive. Sizes divisible by c are therefore systematically richer in tilings, and the effect is enormous: polypons (c = 6) go from 0.0% of 17-pons tiling to 6.9% at n = 18 and back to 0.5% at 19; tetrakis polyaboloes (c = 4) from 0.2% at n = 15 to 7.2% at n = 16, a thirty-six-fold jump between neighbouring sizes. Those three rows were computed after this paragraph was written, which makes them a fair test rather than a fitted story. The classical grids have c = 1 or 2 and almost nothing to fail, which is why their curves are smooth. One caveat kept in view: the drafters (c = 12) do not follow the pattern cleanly — their local peak at n = 14 is not a multiple of 12 — so divisibility gates the opportunity to tile without determining the outcome.

Part IV · the hard shapesThe hat and the turtle aperiodicity evidence

Classifying all 873 free 8-kites left exactly one shape unresolved — and it is the hat, the aperiodic monotile of Smith, Myers, Kaplan and Goodman-Strauss (2023). The engine wasn't told about it; it simply survived every classification the engine knows, which is precisely the computational signature of aperiodicity Myers describes:

  • It tiles locally: surrounds (coronas) exist to depth 6 and counting — a certified Heesch lower bound of 6, with no failure in sight (the true value is ∞, since it tiles the plane).
  • But never periodically: exhaustive search over all candidate wallpaper subgroups finds no tiling with isohedral number k for any k ≤ 14 (originally k ≤ 7 by exact-k backtracking; the SAT exclusion engine later swept k ≤ 14 in thirty seconds — positive controls: the engine finds the 4-anisohedral nonomino and 3-anisohedral kites).

A shape that surrounds indefinitely but admits no k-isohedral tiling at any searched k is either aperiodic or extraordinarily anisohedral — Myers's programs would "run for ever" on it; ours reports it unresolved and hands over exactly this evidence. The same signature has since singled out the turtle among the 10-kites (its numbers sit with the polykite table) — the engine has now rediscovered both of the known aperiodic polykites blind.

Isohedral number 10 — the project record, on nine triangles. The n = 9 row's lone holdout survived every sweep: no tiling with k ≤ 4, and unlike almost every other unresolved shape in the project it also refused to be proven a non-tiler — its surrounds run to depth 6 and show no sign of stopping. That combination is the star's signature, and for a few hours it was the project's new flagship mystery. It is instead the highest-k tile here: the SAT orbit search returns MIN-K = 10, with the cover cross-checked as an exact torus partition (40 tiles per fundamental domain in ten orbits of 36 cells each) and independently re-verified by expanding the 40 representatives back onto the torus — all 360 cells covered exactly once, no duplicates. It is a plain chevron of nine quarter-square triangles, it tiles the plane, and every tiling of it needs at least ten orbits of tiles.

Can the chevron be made honest? That k = 10 is stated for tilings aligned to the tetrakis grid, the convention used throughout this page — and as a free polygon, ignoring the grid, the chevron is merely isohedral. The standard repair is to carve the boundary: replace straight edges with interlocking profiles so that only the intended contacts fit, making alignment a consequence of the shape rather than an assumption. For this tile the repair runs into a specific obstruction, and it is worth stating because it is not obvious.

Of the four marking types, only three can be carved at all (see the taxonomy): jigsaw tabs, S-tabs, and lopsided asymmetric tabs. Of those, the S-tab is the only one that distinguishes a tile from its mirror image — an S meets an S, while a reflected tile presents a Z, and the two refuse. That is exactly the property one wants for pinning down placements. But the chevron's minimal tiling is half reflected: of the forty tiles in its fundamental domain, twenty are direct and twenty are mirror images, and the shape does not tile one-sided at all — banning reflections kills every tiling with k ≤ 12. So on every edge where the tiling mates a tile with its own reflection, an S-tab is inadmissible, and only mirror-blind jigsaw tabs remain — precisely the notch that cannot tell the two apart. The carving that would enforce alignment is the carving that destroys the tiling.

Marking only some edges is the natural escape — leave the mirror-meeting edges plain and carve the rest — so it is worth reporting exactly why that fails too. In the 10-orbit tiling every one of the chevron's eleven edges meets a reflected copy somewhere, so no edge admits an S-tab. Worse, the edges are promiscuous: edge 1 must mate with edges 2, 3 and 10 in the same chirality and with 5, 8 and 9 reflected — six different partners — and every other edge accepts several. A carved profile on edge 1 would have to interlock with six distinct profiles and their mirror images at once, which forces it to be the straight edge. For this tiling, carving cannot work at all.

So the chevron's k = 10 stays grid-qualified, and the honest record for an unmarked polygon belongs elsewhere — to the 9-anisohedral 11-hex above. Polyhexes carry no caveat at all, by an argument that applies to no other family here: any tiling by a polyhex decomposes into the hexagons it is built from, and the honeycomb is the unique tiling of the plane by regular hexagons, so every copy is forced onto the same grid. Alignment is not a convention for polyhexes, it is a theorem. k = 9 is therefore a genuine, unqualified anisohedral number for a plain polygon, while the chevron's 10 is a statement about tetrakis-aligned tilings. The distinction is worth keeping sharp: for polyiamonds it can go either way — triangles tile in shifted rows, so alignment must be argued shape by shape — and for the grids built from several cells per unit it is a convention throughout, exactly as in the classical polyomino literature.

The chevron and a minimal tiling of it, coloured by orbit — ten classes of tile. The previous record in this project was 8 (two 10-hexes and a 13-iamond, found the same night); before that, 6. Drawing this figure exposed a rendering bug worth recording: a square window of sublattice coefficients covers only a thin sliver of the plane when the lattice basis is skewed, so the first renders showed a correct tiling full of phantom holes — the same Gauss-reduction pitfall the decorated validator hit in July, in a new place.

The k ≥ 6 club is quoted above as forty-nine records across eight Laves families, and that count is doing less work than it looks. A reader noticing that several of the k = 6 tilings resemble one another prompted the check, and they were right twice over, for two different reasons.

Some are the same polygon. The grids are not independent: two triakis triangles make a rhombille rhombus, two drafters make a kite, the kisrhombille is the barycentric subdivision of the hexagonal tiling. Two bugs fell out of drawing it. The first picture came out visibly skewed, because the three renderers each kept their own copy of the list of backends whose coordinates live in a 60 degree axial basis, and the three had drifted apart: kisrhombille was square-embedded in all of them and triakis in two. The second was hiding underneath: the sublattice basis was being Gauss-reduced in code coordinates as if they were orthonormal. Reduction now uses the backend's own metric, the frame is specified in translational periods (two, by default) rather than as a fraction of a hand-picked window, and the window is derived from the frame — including the fundamental domain's own reach, since a domain with the area of one lattice cell can still be long and non-convex enough to bite the edge of the picture.

The reasoning was that a rich grid reaches a given k at smaller n — true, and the record is evidence for it: the 10-anisohedral chevron needs only nine cells on a c = 4 grid where a polyhex needs sixteen for the same k. From that it seemed to follow that coprime sizes on the richest grids were the enriched hunting ground. They are not, past small n. A rich grid packs its constraint into few cells, and by the time a shape has twenty of them the boundary is so long that almost nothing tiles at all: the population collapses to non-tilers before it can collapse to interesting tilers.

So the enriched region is small n on rich grids — which is precisely the region already swept exhaustively — or large n on the plainest grids, which is where Myers looked and where the sizes needed (16-hexes and beyond) are past what enumeration reaches. That is the honest shape of the record problem: not a compute budget, but a search space with no unexplored cheap corner left in it.

Tested, and it holds. Polyhexes are the best case for this argument — the highest anisohedral density of any family, and no alignment caveat — so n = 13 was swept to see whether the frontier still pays there. Which makes the shape of the polyhex record clearer, and stranger. The maxima by size run 8 at n = 10, 9 at n = 11, 5 at n = 12, 5 at n = 13 — and then, per Myers, 10 somewhere out at n = 16. High isohedral numbers are not a trend that builds with size; they are isolated shapes that happen to exist at particular sizes, with ordinary rows on either side. The 9-anisohedral 11-hex is not the start of a climb, and neither, presumably, is the 16-hex. That is a discouraging fact for a search strategy based on going one size further, and it is the clearest thing this project has learned about where records live.

Part IV · structureHigh-k tiles are isolated a refuted hypothesis

Every record on this page arrived the same way: enumerate an entire size, classify everything, then probe whatever the sweep could not settle. That is an expensive habit, and the obvious alternative is to search locally — take a shape known to be hard and perturb it, on the theory that anisohedral difficulty is a property of a boundary and so should survive small edits. If true it would replace enumeration with hill-climbing.

It is false, and cheaply so. Taking all twenty-eight known shapes of isohedral number six or more and generating every shape one cell away — add a cell, or remove one while keeping the shape connected — gives 816 neighbours. Their spectrum: 580 do not tile at all, 220 are plainly isohedral, 13 reach k = 2, three reach k = 3, and nothing goes higher. Of the neighbours that tile, ninety-three per cent are ordinary. Removing one triangle from a tile that needs ten orbits does not yield a tile that needs nine; it yields a tile that needs one, or none.

So the hard tiles are isolated points, not clusters, and that has consequences worth stating. It explains why exhaustive enumeration keeps being the only tool that finds them — no record here came from a targeted search. It rules out evolutionary or gradient-style search over shape space, which needs a neighbourhood signal to climb. And combined with the size-coprimality effect, which is predictive of where such shapes live, the working picture is: high-k tiles are sparse and scattered inside identifiable regions, so the leverage lies entirely in choosing which region to enumerate — never in local moves once you are there. The experiment cost twenty-five minutes and retired a whole family of strategies.

Part V · decorationsWang decorations: bump/dent matching rules third audit closed · v3 oracle-verified

The engine supports edge decorations: every boundary edge of a shape carries a label — 0 is neutral (matches itself), labels 1 and 2 are bump/dent partners that match only each other, exactly the Wang-tile mechanism behind the Spectre’s notches. The question this machinery answers: what do decorations do to a shape’s isohedral number? It took three audits to earn a trustworthy answer.

How this section was wrong twice. The first release showed a decorated L-tromino “4-anisohedral” tiling with overlapping bump discs; a reader flagged it, and the overlaps turned out to be bump-meets-bump contacts in a phantom tiling — the validator’s translate window missed touching pairs under skewed sublattice bases (too permissive). The v2 rerun fixed that and was promptly caught the other way: a reader observed that the new “4-orbit” figure looked transitive, and brute force agreed — orbit count 1. Part V · decorationsThe complete taxonomy of edge markings four marking types

The arrow rule is not an ad-hoc variant: it is one of exactly four possibilities, and working out why says what the whole decorated-tiling game consists of. A marking on a shared edge is recorded by each adjacent tile in its own frame — boundary traversal direction, interior on the left. Neighbouring frames differ by ρ, the half-turn about the edge midpoint, so “these two tiles fit” means exactly your record is mine turned by ρ; and placing a tile by a reflection acts on records by σ⊥, reflection across the edge's perpendicular bisector. So the symmetry subgroup of the marking's own profile, inside {1, ρ, σ∥, σ⊥}, determines the matching rule completely:

Which of these can be a shape, and which can only be a marking? A reader raised the question that reorders this whole section: a decoration is only geometry if the matching rule can be carved into the tile's boundary, and not all four types can be. Replacing a straight edge by a simple curve gives a profile whose symmetry class is exactly what the taxonomy above classifies — so ask which symmetries a curve can have. A jigsaw tab (symmetric across the perpendicular bisector) is the familiar puzzle-piece bump. An S-tab (carried to itself by the half-turn about the edge midpoint) is an S-curve, bulging out and then back. A fully asymmetric tab is just a lopsided bump. But the arrow rule asks for a profile symmetric across the edge's own line — and a simple curve spanning two points that is invariant under reflection in the chord joining them must be that chord: anything bulging to one side has its mirror bulging to the other, and the boundary doubles back through itself. An arrow cannot be carved. It can only be painted on, or built from an extra tile.

Every ceiling here has now been tested two orbits higher than it was found. A spectrum that stops at k = 2 because the sweep stopped at kmax 4 is not the same claim as a spectrum that stops at k = 2 with k = 3 through 8 excluded, and until this morning most of the numbers on this page were the first kind. A sweep-wide economy. Every sweep above was, until this morning, re-searching decorations it had already answered: two decorations related by a symmetry of the shape, or by a global relabelling that commutes with the pairing and mirror maps, pose the same problem and must have the same minimum k. Quotienting by both groups before searching costs a few seconds of bookkeeping and cuts the work by 14× on the hexagon and 38× on the four-label scheme — verified by reproducing the arrow-hexagon spectrum exactly, decoration for decoration. Sweeps that had been priced at four hours now run in fifteen minutes, which is what made the four-label sweeps affordable at all.

Postscript on the oracle itself (1 August). While making these sweeps faster, the oracle's own command-line driver turned out to be rebuilding each decoration without its mirror map — harmless for the symmetric-notch sweeps it was written for, but it would have declared every arrow, S-tab and four-label result a violation had it been the tool used on them. It wasn't: those verdicts were produced by drivers that passed the mirror correctly. The driver is now fixed, and every mirror-swapping sweep on this page has been re-checked through it — arrow hexagon 138 positives, five-label arrow 612, four-label 108, S-tab hexagon 12, S-tab L-tromino 38, zero violations. No number here moved; the checker was broken, not the results. It is recorded because a verification tool that silently passes the wrong problem is the most dangerous object in a project like this one.

A methodological coda: v1’s bug could only accept too much, v2’s could only reject too much, and each produced a plausible-looking mathematical narrative — spectra with “gaps”, a special hexagon — that dissolved under an independent check the search code had no hand in. The surviving facts are duller and firmer: rigidity everywhere, one honest doubling, and a pre-filter lemma that does more than the search does.

Part VII · new groundOff the Laves tilings: an Archimedean substrate new family, new lemma

Every backend so far has been a Laves tiling — one cell shape, repeated. The trihexagonal tiling [3.6.3.6] is Archimedean instead, and its cells come in two shapes: regular hexagons and equilateral triangles, two triangles to each hexagon. A polyform on it is a connected union of both kinds, which is a different sort of object from anything above.

The engine took it without modification, which was the pleasant surprise. Nothing in the classifier assumed the cells were congruent — vert_templates was already indexed per residue — so only the backends had made that assumption. The construction is the medial of the honeycomb: its vertices are the honeycomb's edge midpoints, hexagons come from honeycomb faces and triangles from honeycomb vertices, which in the tripled axial coordinates the other backends use puts the three residues at (0,0), (1,1) and (2,2) with vertices on a doubly-fine lattice. The point group is the full D6 about a hexagon centre; a sixth-turn exchanges the two triangle orientations, which is fine because it still maps cells to cells.

A counting lemma does most of the work. A grid-aligned tiling covers every cell exactly once, so the shape's cells must be distributed across residue orbits exactly as the grid is. The trihexagonal tiling has a hexagon orbit and a two-triangle orbit, so a tile must have twice as many triangles as hexagons — and therefore a size divisible by three. Two thirds of all shapes are eliminated by a handful of integer divisions, before any search runs. It is the exact analogue of the bump/dent parity lemma for decorations, and on every Laves backend it is vacuous, because there the cells form a single orbit. That is why it never came up until now.

Part VI · method & performanceHow the engine works

One engine, eleven grids. Every base tiling is described combinatorially: cells are integer points, the tiling's symmetries act as affine integer maps, and a backend only supplies the codes, matrices and cell polygons (adjacency is derived and the group axioms are machine-checked — no floating point anywhere). A periodic tiling by a shape P with symmetry group G ⊆ W collapses to a finite statement: the images of P under G's finitely many coset representatives must exactly partition the torus of cells modulo G's translation sublattice. So tiling questions become exact-cover problems over enumerated subgroups — one orbit for isohedral, k orbits for k-isohedral — and non-tiling is proved separately by Myers's neighbour-pruning, corona elimination and depth-d surround search. This differs from Myers's own class-based backtracking implementation, which makes the replication a genuinely independent check of his tables.

The SAT pivot. Both hard search regimes turned out to be CDCL-solver territory. Surround existence: placements become Boolean variables; each grid cell gets an at-most-one constraint over the placements covering it; corona membership is forced level by level, so “a depth-d surround exists” is one satisfiability query over a placement universe provably closed under everything the recursive search could reach. The star's depth-3 question — hours of inconclusive backtracking — became a 0.2-second SAT call, and its depth-4 refutation a 7-second UNSAT. Periodic exclusion: per candidate group, orbit masks become variables, every torus cell gets an exactly-one constraint, and a cardinality bound at the group's admissible k makes one UNSAT query exclude every k at once; the star's k-exclusion moved from 4.3 CPU-hours (k ≤ 15) to seven minutes (k ≤ 18). Positive answers decode to concrete tilings and are re-verified independently; negative answers emit DRAT proof logs checked by drat-trim, so refutations no longer rest on trusting any search code — ours or the solver's.

Part VI · method & performanceTwo algorithms, measured against each other 1 762 shapes, zero disagreements

This project's engine and Myers's differ at the root. He runs a class-based backtracking search: start with one tile, branch over every way a neighbour can touch an unsurrounded tile, let it join a known class or open a new one, propagate the tiles the resulting symmetries force, and stop when every class representative is surrounded — the symmetry group is never enumerated, it is discovered. We enumerate candidate wallpaper groups up front and solve an exact cover on each quotient torus. Agreement between the two has been this page's main external check, so it was worth asking the sharper question: which is actually faster, and why? A faithful reimplementation of his method now lives beside ours (myers_isohedral.py), verdict-for-verdict identical on all 1 762 free polyominoes at n = 7, 8, 9 — zero disagreements, with every structure it accepts independently developed into a patch and re-verified as an exact torus partition.

The aggregate hides the interesting structure, because the two engines win at opposite ends. On the median shape ours is 4–7× faster: the whole answer is one tiny torus cover, while the class search has to build a patch. In the tail his is faster by up to 14 600×, and the tail owns the total. The reason is structural rather than incidental: a shape that cannot tile dies in his search after a few dozen nodes, whereas we pay a fixed toll — every candidate group at every admissible torus index, restarted for each k — that does not shrink at all for hopeless shapes. The 4-anisohedral nonomino, our 20-second showpiece, takes him 0.28 s. And the gap widens with n, which is the honest explanation of how his tables reach the 25-ominoes.

What our side keeps is the shape of the answer rather than the speed: “no tiling with k ≤ K” is a finite enumeration for us — finitely many groups, each a finite exact cover — which is exactly why it could be handed to a SAT solver and come back with a DRAT certificate, while a backtracking refutation is an unreplayable trace. The group-theoretic formulation also carries to all eleven Laves grids with no per-family reasoning, and decorations ride along on the orbits for free. The natural conclusion is a hybrid rather than a winner: route by difficulty, exact cover first and the class search as tail-breaker, and import two of his devices we cannot currently exploit — possible-neighbour bitmaps used inside the search, and forced-tile propagation read as unit propagation over class-relative surround variables.

Part VI · method & performanceThe speed work ~100× on classification

Classification is ~100× faster than 31 July (pocket-check algorithmics ×12, compiled neighbour kernel ×8–11); decoration sweeps a further 4.5–23.6× on top, all algorithmic; the k-orbit search compiled at 10.4× in isolation. The C enumerator runs at 125–165M shapes/s and produced the first n = 11 and n = 12 omino rows.

Is there another 100×? Yes — and finding out where took a wrong turn worth recording, because the first answer this page gave was measured wrong.

Benchmarking at frontier sizes rather than small ones matters, because the class mix shifts hard: at n = 11 polyhexes 81% of shapes are non-tilers, where small-n benchmarks are mostly tilers. A tiler exits the pipeline at the first stage that succeeds; a non-tiler pays for every stage, because each must fail before the next runs. And exhaustive failure is the expensive case for exact cover: it tries every candidate group at every admissible torus index, a toll that does not shrink for hopeless shapes. So the first move is to run the cheaper discriminator first.

Where the next 10× actually is. Profiling prove_nontiler at n = 11 splits it 57% corona enumeration / 42% surround search — and both are plain interpreted Python, spending 44 million function calls on 91 shapes (5.4M iso_apply, 6.1M mat_vec, 11.2M len). That is the same profile the neighbour context and cover kernels had before they were compiled for 8–16× apiece. The bottleneck is not the algorithm, and it is not the engine choice: it is that the one stage which now dominates everything is the one stage never moved to C.

So it was taken. Profiling prove_nontiler put ~57% of it in coronas, a Python recursion doing bigint AND on 260-bit masks — 3.4M recursive calls for 91 shapes. The algorithm was already right; only the arithmetic was slow. Masks reach ~442 bits, so they became seven-word uint64 bitsets and each disjointness test became a few word ANDs. The kernel mirrors the reference branch-for-branch, including the odd overflow rule (append, then test len > cap, so cap=0 reports overflow the moment one corona exists); verified identical in contents and order over 1807 comparisons across three families at several caps, zero mismatches. That alone is 13× on coronas.

Then the surround search, which the corona kernel promoted to the bottleneck. Two changes, both about not recomputing what is already known. try_place re-derived the same tile image every time a candidate isometry was retried on another branch, so the cell image, ring image and vertex-neighbour lookup are now memoised per call. And it found the touching tiles by scanning every cell's vertex neighbours — about 11 × 12 dict probes — when nb.ring is by construction the tile's external vertex-neighbour halo, and the overlap scan just above has already established that no new cell is owned. Any owned neighbour must therefore lie in the ring image: the same set in ~24 probes.

A second contention casualty, caught this time. Midway through, the n = 12 polyhex row was launched on ten cores; the very next benchmark reported prove_nontiler at 24.1 ms, twice its true value, and the “speedup” of a change that had not yet been written to disk. Same failure that produced the 6.88× above, one section earlier, with the diagnosis already written down. The row was cancelled — 683,101 shapes extrapolating to four to six hours — and re-measured idle. Benchmarks and sweeps cannot share a machine, and knowing that is apparently not the same as remembering it.

Part VI · method & performanceThe audit trail four soundness episodes

This page's numbers have survived four soundness episodes, two of them caught by a reader of the figures, and it seems more honest to display that history than to bury it. (Episodes 2 and 3 are told from the decorations' side in Part V.) (1) An instrumentation ambiguity let node-budget cap-hits be read as found surrounds — caught in an internal audit; every claim was re-derived and the reporting disambiguated. (2) The decorated-tiling validator checked label compatibility over a fixed window of lattice translates in Hermite coordinates; skewed bases let genuinely touching tiles escape the window, and phantom decorated tilings slipped through — caught by a reader noticing overlapping bump discs, which turned out to be bump-meets-bump contacts no valid tiling could contain. Fixed with a Gauss-reduced basis, a span-derived window, and an independent patch-level post-check on every decorated success. (3) Decorated placements were identified by their cell footprint, which conflates mirror aspects whose labels differ; the decoder could pick the wrong aspect and reject genuine covers, inflating decorated isohedral numbers — caught by a reader observing that a published “4-orbit” tiling looked transitive (it was; brute force confirms orbit count 1). The v3 engine keys placements by label class and verifies every positive against a brute-force orbit-count oracle. (4) The SAT cross-checks exposed that a wrap-around placement whose torus footprint is merely setwise-stabilized can enter an “orbit” whose plane expansion double-covers — a false-positive-only risk; the systematic re-audit came back clean (all 37 stored anisohedral examples re-derived exactly). The pattern worth stating: every bug was found because some independent artifact — a figure, a cross-check, a second engine — contradicted the search, which is the strongest argument this project knows for computing everything twice.
