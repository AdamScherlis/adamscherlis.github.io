What the high-k tilings look like as a set

They are worth looking at as a set. The high-k tilings are not subtle variations on a theme — some are stripes, some are pinwheels, some are long interlocking chevrons, and the 6-orbit polyrhombilles alone run through most of that range. What they share is that no arrangement with fewer orbits exists, which is exactly the thing the pictures cannot show you.

Ordered by isohedral number, then family. The first is the chevron at ten orbits, the second the 11-hex at nine — the record and the honest record. The three shapes whose tiling structure could not be classified (Part IV) are still drawn here; only their combinatorial type is unknown, not their tiling.

Written for the hand-curated 31-tiling gallery; the autogenerated galleries that replaced it draw all 272 anisohedral tilings at k ≥ 4. A related fact that lived beside it: the 62 club shapes reduce to 49 distinct polygons under boundary congruence — a one-sided 11-cell polyrhombille and a 22-cell polypon trace the same outline, as do a polypon and its one-sided twin at n = 10 (count_club.py --gallery derives this).

Reflections are load-bearing

Moved from the records section, 9 August 2026. The survey of one-sided isohedral numbers on the c = 1 grids, and the patch-filling trap it re-sprang.

The same question, asked of every hard shape on those two grids. If one-sided isohedral numbers can only be larger, the natural hope is that some shape gains orbits when its mirror is removed — ideally landing on the missing seven. All thirteen shapes at k = 5 or 6 on the honeycomb and the square lattice were re-classified under direct isometries, with anything the k-search could not settle taken to a SAT patch at 12, 16, 20 and 24 cells square. The answer is that eleven of the thirteen stop tiling altogether — proven one-sided non-tilers — and the remaining two keep the isohedral number they already had, five staying five and six staying six. Not one gains an orbit.

That is a sharper statement than it first looks. Reflections in these tilings are never a mild convenience that a chiral tile could pay for with a few extra transitivity classes; they are either irrelevant or load-bearing, and when they are load-bearing their removal does not cost orbits, it costs the tiling. Thirteen shapes, and the middle case — a tile that still tiles but needs more classes to do it — does not occur once. The last of the thirteen is also the best warning against optimism here. It is a 13-hex that admits no one-sided tiling with k ≤ 12 and fills patches of 12, 16, 20 and 24 cells square without complaint — which reads exactly like a tile of enormous one-sided isohedral number. It is not. At 28×28 the formula goes UNSAT and it joins the non-tilers. Two other shapes in the same batch died at 20×20 after filling 16×16. “Fills a large patch” is weak evidence about the plane, and how weak depends on a scale nothing tells you in advance.

Do the records survive a mirror ban? One-sided isohedral numbers can only be larger than free ones — forbidding reflections removes symmetries a tiling might have leaned on, so a shape needing nine orbits with mirrors could need more without them. Since polyhex results carry no alignment caveat either way, a one-sided polyhex above ten would be an honest record in its own category. So it was worth asking of the two best shapes here.

The exclusion search said k = 1…12 all impossible for both, which looks exactly like a record and is not one. Neither shape tiles at all without reflections. A 12×12 patch is UNSAT under direct isometries for the 9-anisohedral 11-hex and for the 8-anisohedral 10-hex, so their one-sided isohedral number is not thirteen-or-more; it does not exist. The engine already knew — its ordinary corona exhaustion refutes both in milliseconds under the direct view — which is the point worth keeping: an exclusion search reporting “no tiling with k ≤ 12” and a shape that simply cannot tile produce the same output. That is the conflation Part VII was built to break, met here from the opposite direction, in the middle of a record hunt. These tiles are essentially reflective: the mirror is not decoration on their tilings, it is load-bearing.

Can the chevron be made honest?

Moved from the records section, 9 August 2026. Why no carvable marking rescues the k = 10 tetrakis chevron from its grid qualifier.

Can the chevron be made honest? That k = 10 is stated for tilings aligned to the tetrakis grid, the convention used throughout this page — and as a free polygon, ignoring the grid, the chevron is merely isohedral. The standard repair is to carve the boundary: replace straight edges with interlocking profiles so that only the intended contacts fit, making alignment a consequence of the shape rather than an assumption. For this tile the repair runs into a specific obstruction, and it is worth stating because it is not obvious.

Of the four marking types, only three can be carved at all (see the taxonomy): jigsaw tabs, S-tabs, and lopsided asymmetric tabs. Of those, the S-tab is the only one that distinguishes a tile from its mirror image — an S meets an S, while a reflected tile presents a Z, and the two refuse. That is exactly the property one wants for pinning down placements. But the chevron's minimal tiling is half reflected: of the forty tiles in its fundamental domain, twenty are direct and twenty are mirror images, and the shape does not tile one-sided at all — banning reflections kills every tiling with k ≤ 12. So on every edge where the tiling mates a tile with its own reflection, an S-tab is inadmissible, and only mirror-blind jigsaw tabs remain — precisely the notch that cannot tell the two apart. The carving that would enforce alignment is the carving that destroys the tiling.

Marking only some edges is the natural escape — leave the mirror-meeting edges plain and carve the rest — so it is worth reporting exactly why that fails too. In the 10-orbit tiling every one of the chevron's eleven edges meets a reflected copy somewhere, so no edge admits an S-tab. Worse, the edges are promiscuous: edge 1 must mate with edges 2, 3 and 10 in the same chirality and with 5, 8 and 9 reflected — six different partners — and every other edge accepts several. A carved profile on edge 1 would have to interlock with six distinct profiles and their mirror images at once, which forces it to be the straight edge. For this tiling, carving cannot work at all.

So the chevron's k = 10 stays grid-qualified, and the honest record for an unmarked polygon belongs elsewhere — to the 9-anisohedral 11-hex above. Polyhexes carry no caveat at all, by an argument that applies to no other family here: any tiling by a polyhex decomposes into the hexagons it is built from, and the honeycomb is the unique tiling of the plane by regular hexagons, so every copy is forced onto the same grid. Alignment is not a convention for polyhexes, it is a theorem. k = 9 is therefore a genuine, unqualified anisohedral number for a plain polygon, while the chevron's 10 is a statement about tetrakis-aligned tilings. The distinction is worth keeping sharp: for polyiamonds it can go either way — triangles tile in shifted rows, so alignment must be argued shape by shape — and for the grids built from several cells per unit it is a convention throughout, exactly as in the classical polyomino literature.

The last sixteen decorations

Moved from Part VII, 9 August 2026. The mechanism paragraphs it condenses are on the algorithms page; this is the endgame story and the DRAT caveat.

It does not, and for a structural reason. The corona machinery assumes every tile has the same set of legal neighbours — true for a plain polyform, false for a decorated one, where a tile in a different aspect presents different labels and so admits a different set of neighbours. Its candidate lists are indexed by neighbour cell-set, which cannot express a condition on orientation at all. Pushed through it anyway, a decoration is refuted only when the undecorated shape already is, and for a hexagon or a dihex that is never. Corona exhaustion proves exactly nothing here, at any depth.

The strip transfer matrix. Suppose the decorated tile tiles the plane. Restrict that tiling to a vertical strip of w columns: each row carries a state — which aspect covers each of its w cells — from a finite set, and consecutive rows must be compatible. So the tiling induces a bi-infinite path through a finite directed graph, and a finite graph carries one exactly when repeatedly deleting states with no successor, or no predecessor, leaves something behind. If that fixpoint empties, no tiling exists at all, for any k. Unlike a kmax sweep it does not stop at some isohedral number; unlike a corona proof it is not confined to a bounded neighbourhood. Soundness runs one way, which is the useful way: the strip’s border cells carry no constraint against the world outside, so the graph is too permissive, never too strict. It can fail to prove; it cannot wrongly prove.

The cost is small because the geometry factors out. For a one-cell tile the whole transition relation is three A × A tables, one per lattice direction, so geometry is touched A² times and the rest is bitwise work on Python integers. Multi-cell shapes need the alphabet refined from “which aspect” to “which aspect, and which of the shape’s own cells sits here”, which pins the covering copy exactly — any other placement covering that cell would overlap, which the matching test already rejects. And rows are grown through the compatibility graph rather than sieved from the A^w product, which at width 5 on a hexagon is 7,488 rows instead of 248,832. That settles 95,842 of the 95,934, most of them at width 2 or 3.

What the global test cannot see, a local one can. Row sets grow about sixfold per unit of width and the successor tables are quadratic in them, so width 7 on a dihex would want ~77 GB: the transfer matrix simply stops. The remainder goes to two other instruments. Patch exhaustion places tiles outward from the origin keeping every isometry and checks the matching rule between every touching pair, asking only whether a disc can be covered — tiles hang over the boundary freely, since demanding exact coverage of a region would be unsound (an L-tromino tiles the plane and cannot cover a 3×3 square). Then CDCL: give every cell of a w × h patch a variable per symbol, demand exactly one, forbid every incompatible adjacent pair. Any plane tiling restricted to the patch satisfies that formula, so UNSAT is a proof for every k — and a clause-learning solver goes where both hand-rolled searches stop. Between them, 92 decorations the strip could not reach: 52 by patch, 40 by SAT. None are left.

The last forty are the interesting ones, and they are not aperiodic after all. Every one fills an 8×8 patch comfortably, admits no periodic tiling on any torus up to 8×8, and is refuted only at 12×12 — an obstruction at a scale no strip of width 6 or disc of radius 5 could ever have reached. Four independent solvers agree on all sixteen final refutations, and drat-trim certified the proofs it could; pysat returned truncated DRAT on the rest, so those rest on solver agreement rather than a checked proof, which is worth saying plainly rather than implying more rigour than is there.

Two wrong explanations for the missing seven

Moved from Part IV, 9 August 2026. Both were published briefly and refuted by predictions checked against data already on disk.

Why is a harder question, and two tempting answers are both wrong. The first is shadowing: seven-orbit tilings exist, but any shape flexible enough for seven is also coverable in six, so it is filed as a six. That predicts the known k = 6 shapes should mostly admit seven-orbit tilings as well. Zero of twenty-six do. For these shapes, seven-orbit tilings simply do not exist.

The second is arithmetic. For a tile with trivial symmetry — which every high-k shape has — each orbit contributes q tiles to a fundamental domain, so the divisibility law becomes c | n · k · q, and a k sharing a factor with c is admissible at every point-group order while a coprime k needs the factor from somewhere else. That is real, and it is not enough. On the rhombille tiling (c = 3) seven is fully admissible whenever three divides n — six admissible (q, torus) pairs at n = 9, 12 and 15, exactly as many as six enjoys — and those are the sizes richest in tilings. Rhombille has produced 21 shapes at k = 6 and none at seven. A constraint that vanishes at a third of all sizes cannot account for a 62-to-0 split.
