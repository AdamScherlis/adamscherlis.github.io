The families

Fifteen families of polyforms, each classified completely up to some size: every shape counted below is in exactly one column.

Columns follow the hierarchy on the overview: shapes with holes; shapes that tile by translation; by translations and half-turns but not translation alone; by some other isohedral tiling; anisohedral shapes, broken down by isohedral number; and proved non-tilers. “Tile %” is the share of hole-free shapes that tile. Isohedral numbers of four or more link to pictures of the tilings. A ✓ means every column of that row equals the corresponding entry in Myers's tables. One-sided tables count mirror images as different shapes and forbid reflected copies in the tiling.

The reference family, and the gentlest grid: the share of polyominoes that tile falls slowly and smoothly with size. Anisohedral polyominoes are almost all 2-anisohedral; the single 6-anisohedral 14-omino is the only polyomino here above k = 5. No polyomino of fifteen cells or fewer has isohedral number seven.

The richest of the classical families in hard tiles: two 10-hexes need eight orbits and one 11-hex needs nine, all three predicted by Myers's tables and found here independently. 7 429 658 free shapes, n ≤ 15 · 6 cells per translation unit · first non-tiler at n = 2 · first anisohedral at n = 4 · highest isohedral number 6

The family of the hat. Six kites meet at each lattice point, so translation tilings exist only at multiples of six and most sizes are nearly barren: no 10-kite or 11-kite tiles isohedrally at all. Polycairos

Cairo pentagon · Cairo pentagonal tiling [3.3.4.3.4] · new classification

4 776 093 free shapes, n ≤ 13 · 4 cells per translation unit · first non-tiler at n = 5 · first anisohedral at n = 5 · highest isohedral number 4

Pentagons of the Cairo tiling, four to a translation unit. Free counts agree with OEIS A159866. Anisohedral polycairos start at five cells. The grid has a strong parity rhythm: translation and half-turn tilings exist only at even sizes, and every one of the seventeen 4-anisohedral polycairos has an odd number of cells. Nothing in this family has ever been left unresolved by a sweep.

Polyrhombilles

60° rhombus · rhombille tiling [3.6.3.6] · new classification

7 859 224 free shapes, n ≤ 17 · 3 cells per translation unit · first non-tiler at n = 4 · first anisohedral at n = 5 · highest isohedral number 6

60° rhombi, three to a hexagon. This is the family that produces six-orbit tiles in bulk: three of the twelve 5-rhombilles are 6-anisohedral — polyominoes do not reach six until fourteen cells — and at n = 17 no shape tiles isohedrally at all, yet sixteen tile with six orbits. Free anisohedral polyrhombilles need 2, 3 or 6 orbits and never 4 or 5. Every polyrhombille is also a polyiamond, and the hard cases do tile more simply once alignment with the rhombille grid is dropped; the numbers are for aligned tilings.

Polyflorets

floret pentagon · floret pentagonal tiling [3.3.3.3.6] · new classification

4 364 522 free shapes, n ≤ 13 · 6 cells per translation unit · first non-tiler at n = 2 · first anisohedral at n = 9 · highest isohedral number 6 · nothing tiles at n = 5, 11

The floret pentagonal tiling is chiral — it has no mirror symmetry — so its polyforms are one-sided by nature and there is no separate one-sided table. It is a hostile grid: no 5-floret and no 11-floret tiles, and of 55 624 10-florets exactly two do, one with three orbits and one with six. Sizes divisible by six are the exception: at n = 12 several thousand shapes tile, 78 of them with three orbits.

Polyprisms

prismatic pentagon · prismatic pentagonal tiling [3.3.3.4.4] · new classification

5 333 962 free shapes, n ≤ 13 · 2 cells per translation unit · first non-tiler at n = 4 · first anisohedral at n = 5 · highest isohedral number 3

The friendliest of the new families: the single pentagon tiles by half-turns, the first non-tiler appears at four cells, and a healthy share of shapes still tiles at thirteen. Its anisohedral shapes never exceed three orbits. Without reflections the grid's only point symmetry is the half-turn, so one-sided polyprisms can only tile by translation or half-turn, and no one-sided polyprism is anisohedral.

Tetrakis polyaboloes

5 327 372 free shapes, n ≤ 19 · 4 cells per translation unit · first non-tiler at n = 3 · first anisohedral at n = 7 · highest isohedral number 10

Right triangles obtained by cutting each square along both diagonals; free and one-sided counts agree with OEIS A197465 and A197466. Behaviour splits by parity: translation and half-turn tilings occur only at even sizes, while every odd size from seven up has 4-anisohedral shapes and almost nothing isohedral. The family holds the project's record, a nine-cell chevron with isohedral number ten, and the smallest six-orbit tile by area, seven cells covering 1¾ unit squares.

Classical polyaboloes are made of tans, half-square right triangles. The highest isohedral number is four, reached by a single 9-abolo.

Polydrafters

30°–60°–90° triangle · kisrhombille tiling [4.6.12] · new classification

6 408 400 free shapes, n ≤ 19 · 12 cells per translation unit · first non-tiler at n = 3 · first anisohedral at n = 7 · highest isohedral number 6 · nothing tiles at n = 11, 17

30°–60°–90° triangles, twelve to a translation unit — the grid with the most cells per unit and the sparsest tilings. These are edge-to-edge polydrafters. Translation tilings need n divisible by twelve and half-turn tilings n divisible by six. The grid is half left-handed and half right-handed cells, and rotations preserve handedness, so a one-sided shape with unequal numbers of each can never tile: every odd one-sided size is empty. The single unresolved 16-drafter is the hat.

30°–30°–120° triangles, three to each triangle of the triangular grid; the classical polypons. Counts agree with OEIS A057784 (free) and A151531 (one-sided) as far as those sequences go. An Archimedean grid: hexagons and triangles, two triangles to each hexagon. A grid-aligned tiling covers each kind of cell in the grid's proportions, so a tile must contain exactly twice as many triangles as hexagons. Polygems

gem (two kites sharing a long edge: a third of a hexagon) · gems on the deltoidal trihexagonal tiling [3.4.6.4] · a slice of the polykites

148 809 free shapes, n ≤ 8 · 3 cells per translation unit · first non-tiler at n = 2 · first anisohedral at n = 2 · highest isohedral number 6

A gem is two kites that share a long edge: a pentagon, a third of a hexagon. Polygems are polykites, and a thin slice of them — yet most of the hard polykites are in the slice. The hat is one of 78 4-gems (against 873 8-kites) and the turtle one of 450 5-gems (against 10 011 10-kites). Here n counts gems, so each shape has 2n kites.

No polygem of 2, 5 or 8 gems tiles isohedrally. For 5 gems that is true of every 10-kite. For 2 and 8 it is special to polygems — 16-kites that tile isohedrally exist in their thousands — and the reason is short. The area of a 4-kite or 16-kite forces any isohedral tiling to have a centre of six-fold symmetry, or of three-fold symmetry with mirrors, at the centre of some hexagon of the grid, where six tiles meet and take one kite of that hexagon each. A polygem meets every hexagon in whole gems, two kites at a time.
