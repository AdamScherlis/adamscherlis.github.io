Looking for another monotile

The hat and the Spectre are the same tile at different settings of one dial. This page describes a search built around that dial: what it covers, what it found, and where it stops.

One tile, two edge lengths

The hat's edges come in two lengths, and the two kinds never have to agree. Walk round the boundary adding up only the short edges and you return to the start; the same is true of the long ones. So either length can be changed freely and the outline still closes, giving a family of polygons Tile(a, b) with the same angles. The hat is Tile(1, √3) and the turtle Tile(√3, 1). Call a polyform deformable when its boundary has this property.

Where the two lengths are equal the polygon is equilateral, and something changes: an edge of one kind can now lie against an edge of the other, so the tile fits together in ways no member of the family could before. For the hat this point is Tile(1, 1). It tiles periodically if reflected copies are allowed, and aperiodically if they are not — that is the Spectre. The equilateral point is where a deformable family is most likely to do something new, and it is also off every grid: the vertices of Tile(1, 1) lie on no lattice, which is why none of the sweeps elsewhere on this site can see it.

Three branches

An equilateral polygon with all turns a multiple of 30° can be placed in 24 ways up to translation: twelve rotations, each with or without a reflection. Away from the equilateral point only half of them are usable, because a placement must keep short edges on short edges. There are three natural ways to choose half:

  • hat — rotations by multiples of 60°, and the reflections that keep each edge in its class. These are exactly the tilings available to Tile(a, b) for a ≠ b, so a verdict here matches the verdict for the polyform on its own grid.
  • chiral — all twelve rotations and no reflections. Tile(1, 1) here is the Spectre.
  • swap — rotations by multiples of 60°, and the reflections that exchange the two classes.

A tile with no periodic tiling in one branch but a tiling of some kind there is an aperiodic monotile once its edges are shaped to enforce the branch, as the Spectre's curved edges enforce chirality. Polygons with 45° turns have the same three branches, with sixteen placements and rotations by 90°.

What the search found

Every deformable polyform on a grid gives one equilateral polygon. Each was classified in each branch by an exact search that works directly with polygons rather than grid cells: first for a proof that it cannot be surrounded, then for a periodic tiling, which is stored and checked independently.

With 30° turns the search leaves two cases unsettled, and they are the same polygon: Tile(1, 1), in the hat and chiral branches. In the chiral branch that is the Spectre, and the search behaves as it should for an aperiodic tile: it surrounds, and no periodic tiling turns up. In the hat branch Tile(1, 1) inherits the hat's own tilings, so that entry is the hat seen from the equilateral point. In the swap branch the same polygon tiles with two tiles to a period. With 45° turns nothing is left over: every polygon either fails to surround or tiles periodically, in all three branches.

So among polygons that come from polyforms on these two grids, up to the sizes shown, the hat's family is the only one of its kind. As a check on the engine, the hat-branch verdicts were compared with the grid-based classifier, which decides the same question by entirely different code: they agree on every polygon compared. All tilings here are edge to edge, with whole unit edges meeting whole unit edges; the straight vertex in the hat's long side is what lets its tilings be described that way.

The polygons above all come from polyforms on a grid. The same classifier can be pointed at every equilateral polygon of a given number of edges: every simple closed walk of unit steps whose turns are multiples of 30°, 45° or 36°. Most of these come from no polyform at all. Each is tried in the three branches and also with every placement allowed (full), which is the plain question of whether the polygon tiles edge to edge.

Tiles that need every kind of placement

The three branches are the three ways of using half the placements. Among the polygons with 30° turns, 14 tile the plane and yet fail in all three: every tiling by one of them contains copies turned 30° from each other and reflected copies. No tiling of that kind can have all its tiles equivalent, because a symmetry of a periodic tiling cannot turn anything by 30°. The one below, with 7 edges, needs eight tiles to a period; the lattice search missed it, and for an afternoon it was the best candidate on this page. Its period was eventually read straight off a surround, which is now part of the method.

“No” means that some vertex or the first ring of neighbours cannot be completed, or that a surround of some depth is impossible; “tiles” means a periodic tiling was found and checked from scratch. A question mark is not a discovery. It means the polygon can be surrounded to the depth shown and has no periodic tiling among the period lattices tried, which is what an aperiodic tile would look like and also what a tile with a large period or a large Heesch number looks like.

Most question marks do not last. A surround of a rigid tile is a piece of its tiling, and the period can be read off it directly, or the rule by which neighbours meet can be read off and continued until the period appears; tilings with eight tiles to a period in long thin cells were found this way after the search over lattices had missed them. What is left, apart from Tile(1, 1), is the opposite kind of tile: small, nearly convex polygons that fit their neighbours in a great many ways. Their surrounds are arbitrary, so nothing can be read off them, and they are much more likely to tile in some way not yet tried than to be aperiodic.

Two edge lengths

Each family of directions has a second natural edge length, the one that keeps the vertices in the same ring of points: 1 and the golden ratio for 36° turns, as in the Penrose kite and dart; 1 and √2 for 45°; 1 and √3 for 30°. A long edge may only lie against a long edge. Every such polygon was classified under every subgroup of placements.

Two of these families matter more than their size suggests. What makes the hat work is not its angles but that its edges form two classes which close separately, so that it lives in a four-dimensional lattice and comes as a continuous family. The same is true of a rectilinear polygon with two incommensurable edge lengths, and of one whose edges run along the three directions of the triangular lattice; these are the only other families of the kind in which a reflected copy is still the same tile. Their members with a rational ratio of lengths are polyominoes and polyiamond-like shapes, and tilings carry over along a family. An aperiodic tile here would hand polyominoes its tilings. Every way of restricting the placements

The three branches are not the only restrictions worth asking about. Any subgroup of the rotations and reflections can be imposed: translations only, half-turns, one direction of mirror, and so on. A tile that tiles under some restriction, but never periodically, is an aperiodic monotile for that restriction, whether or not it tiles periodically once the restriction is lifted. Some restrictions can be built into the tile itself. Any restriction to rotations can: give each edge a curve that depends on the direction the edge faces, as the Spectre's curved edges build in chirality, and only the permitted turns will fit. The hat's branch is built in by its two edge lengths. For other restrictions that keep some reflections there is not always a way to do it, and the verdict is then a statement about what the polygon can do rather than a tile one could cut out.

So every polygon above that tiles at all was tried under every subgroup. Most verdicts come free: a non-tiler stays one under any restriction, and a periodic tiling found under a restriction is still there when it is lifted.

Decorating the edges

The hat and the Spectre are the same polygon under two different restrictions, and both restrictions are things done to its edges: two kinds of edge that may not be mixed, and edges all curved the same way. Subgroups of placements are a third case. The general notion is an edge decoration. Replace an edge by a curve. The tile across the edge sees that curve turned half round; a reflected copy of the tile carries its mirror image. So what matters about a curve is its own symmetry, and there are three kinds: a curve that looks the same turned half round (an S, or its mirror image Z), which lets a tile meet only neighbours of its own handedness; a symmetric bump, which must lie in a dent; and a curve with no symmetry, which does both. A decorated polygon is an honest tile, and its tilings are exactly those tilings of the plain polygon in which the curves fit. Decoration can only remove tilings. A decorated tile that still tiles, but never periodically, is a monotile.

Before any verdict was used, the search had to reproduce what is known: with an S on every edge it must agree with the rotations-only branch on every polygon (it does), and no decoration that visibly fits a stored periodic tiling may be rejected (none is).

Start with Tile(1, 1) itself. Of its 16 384 ways of putting an S or a Z on each edge, 161 can be surrounded once, 160 of those tile periodically, and one does not: an S everywhere, the Spectre. With bumps and dents, 161 of 16 384 patterns can be surrounded, 160 tile periodically, and again one does not: bumps and dents alternating round the tile. The two survivors are drawn above, and they are the same tile in the sense that matters: each allows exactly the same 35 ways for two copies to sit next to each other, none of them reflected. So the Spectre does not need chiral curves; alternate bumps and dents, each one mirror-symmetric, exclude the reflected tile just as well. The reason is a parity rule that holds for all 81 ways a copy of plain Tile(1, 1) can sit beside another: an unreflected neighbour always meets an odd-numbered edge against an even-numbered one, and a reflected neighbour meets two of the same parity. With two kinds of straight edge, 161 of 16 384 patterns can be surrounded and one is not periodic: the hat's own division of the edges. Nothing else turned up: decorating Tile(1, 1) finds the two known monotiles and no third.

At the other extreme from Tile(1, 1) are the regular polygons, which tile in only one way, so that a decorated one is a single tile on a lattice with nothing to choose but which way round it sits. Here the question can be closed, because the list of decorations is finite: no more kinds of curve can be used than there are edges. A regular triangle has 23 decorations in all; 4 cannot be surrounded once and the other 19 tile periodically, with at most six tiles in a period. A regular square has 133 decorations in all; 29 cannot be surrounded once and the other 104 tile periodically, with at most eight tiles in a period. A regular hexagon has 4 954 decorations in all; 4 073 cannot be surrounded once and the other 881 tile periodically, with at most four tiles in a period. So no regular triangle, square or hexagon becomes an aperiodic monotile by shaping its edges, whatever the curves. (The one known hexagonal monotile, Socolar and Taylor's, uses rules between tiles that do not share an edge, and cannot be cut out as a single piece.)

Fourteen decorated polygons were first left unsettled and then settled — by decorating them further, by a deeper surround, or by a more patient reading of the period, which for some holds twelve tiles — and each such tiling is stored and checked. One remains unsettled: a 30° polygon of 8 edges with the pattern + - + - + - + -, which fits beside itself in 74 ways. That is the profile of a tile whose period has simply not been found, not of a monotile: every earlier case of the kind tiled periodically in the end.

Decoration turned out to be useful for a second reason. Three small polygons had resisted every attempt to settle them: they fit together in so many ways that a patch of their tiling shows no pattern, and the search for a period drowns. A decorated copy of such a polygon has far fewer ways to fit — fifteen instead of eighty for one heptagon — and if it still tiles, its period can be read off a patch. That period is then checked on the plain polygon. All three tile periodically, two of them with eight tiles in a period, so no plain polygon in any of the searches on this page remains unsettled except Tile(1, 1) and its family.

What arithmetic says

Sweeping every polygon is honest and slow. A little mathematics goes further. The edges of one of these polygons come in two classes, those in even and those in odd multiples of 30°, and the outline closes in each class separately. So a tiling can be redrawn with the two classes scaled independently — that is the family Tile(a, b) — and even with each class turned as well as scaled. However it is redrawn, the tiles of one period still cover the period exactly once, and comparing areas across all the redrawings gives an identity:

a number computed from the tile and from how many copies of each kind a period holds  =  four times a number of the form p² + pq + q².

It holds, exactly, for every periodic tiling on this page. For Tile(1, 1), whose two extreme redrawings are polyiamonds of 8 and 4 triangles, the left side is a multiple of a² − 7ab + b² for a unreflected and b reflected copies, and of a² − 8ab + b² for a copies in even and b in odd rotations. These vanish at a/b = φ⁴ and at 4 + √15: the proportions of reflected hats in a hat tiling, and of odd Spectres in a Spectre tiling. A tiling with threefold symmetry on average must sit exactly on such a zero, and no period can, the zeros being irrational. That is the arithmetic shape of a hat-like tile, and it can be read off a polygon without drawing a single neighbour.

Where no threefold rotation is allowed the identity sharpens to an equation for the proportions themselves: under quarter-turns, a periodic tiling by Tile(1, 1) would need its even and odd copies in a ratio solving a² − 38ab + b² = 0, which no whole numbers do. In those branches a polygon whose equation has only irrational solutions cannot tile periodically at all, and the only question left is whether it tiles.

A second, simpler fact sorts the polygons with the hat's turns into three types. Corners of 90° and 270° never meet corners of 120° and 240° at a vertex, and counting what must pair with what leaves only three possibilities for a tile that tiles: six net turns of 60° and no net right angles, three and two, or none and four. Call them hexagonal, mixed and square. Tile(1, 1) is mixed.

Polygems

The hat is four gems, a gem being two kites joined along a long edge — a pentagon, one third of a hexagon — and the turtle is five. Building shapes from gems instead of kites thins the search enormously, and the question is whether it throws away what we are looking for. Among the shapes already classified it does not:

The limit on this route is growth: polygems still multiply by about six with each gem added, and Myers's polykite tables already reach 25 kites, so getting past known ground with gems alone would mean thirteen gems and around a billion shapes. Larger building blocks — unions of several kites that themselves tile — thin the search further, and are the natural next step. Polygem tables.

Where this goes

  • More edges. The number of polygons grows about sevenfold with every two edges for the hat's turns, and faster with all turns allowed. Fourteen edges is where the Spectre sits, so the next sizes are the first that nobody has a reason to think are empty.
  • The question marks. Every unsettled polygon gets deeper surrounds and larger periods until it tiles, fails, or earns a closer look.
  • More than two edge classes. A boundary that closes separately in three classes has a two-parameter family and more degenerate points, where two or all three lengths coincide.
  • Bigger building blocks. Polyforms made from a tiler of several kites, to carry the polygem idea past the sizes already tabulated.
