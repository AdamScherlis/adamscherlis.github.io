Hard shapes

Almost every shape is easy: it tiles in an obvious way or fails for an obvious local reason. This page is about the rest — the tiles that need many orbits, the non-tilers that nearly tile, and the few that tile without any periodicity at all.

Aperiodic monotiles

A sweep classifies each shape with bounded effort and reports what it cannot settle. Everything it leaves is then probed much harder, and across 81 682 298 shapes only three survive both: they can be surrounded to every depth tried, and have no periodic tiling with any number of orbits tried.

The first is the hat, the aperiodic monotile of Smith, Myers, Kaplan and Goodman-Strauss (2023): it has no tiling with fourteen or fewer orbits and surrounds to depth six and beyond. The second is the turtle, from the same paper, with no tiling up to fourteen orbits and surrounds to depth seven. The third is the hat again. Every kite is two cells of the kisrhombille grid, so the hat is also a 16-drafter, and the polydrafter sweep found it with no knowledge of the kite result: surrounds to depth twelve, no tiling with thirty or fewer orbits, and congruent to the hat by comparison of boundary polygons.

This is evidence, not proof — aperiodicity is established in the paper, not here. What the computation shows is that the method singles these shapes out and nothing else: there is no other candidate aperiodic monotile among the shapes classified, on any of the fifteen grids. With reflections forbidden, both the hat and the turtle are proved non-tilers, in agreement with the known fact that their tilings need mirror images.

Two patterns stand out. The grids with several cells per translation unit — rhombille, triakis, kite, tetrakis — produce six-orbit tiles in quantity and have never produced a five-orbit one, while all thirteen free shapes at k = 5 are polyominoes, polyhexes or polyiamonds. And no shape of any family has isohedral number seven. Both are discussed under open problems.

Six orbits or more

Sixty-seven shapes need at least six orbits: one at k = 10, one at k = 9, three at k = 8, sixty-two at k = 6. For each, every smaller number of orbits has been excluded by exhaustive search and a tiling with exactly k orbits has been constructed and checked. Click a shape for its tiling.

The count overstates the variety. The grids are related — two pons make a rhombus, two drafters make a kite — so one polygon can appear in several families, and a one-sided entry is the same polygon as its free version. Comparing boundary polygons, the sixty-seven entries are fifty distinct polygons; entries marked “≅” share a polygon with another entry. A shape's isohedral number is still a statement about its own grid: a finer grid admits more tilings, so the same polygon can honestly carry different numbers on different grids.

These are grid-aligned numbers. For the polyhexes they are unconditional. For the other families a shape may have simpler tilings that ignore its grid, and whether it does can be tested: the grids refine one another (a rhombus is two triangles, a kite two drafters), and two of them — tetrakis and kisrhombille — contain smaller, turned copies of themselves, so the same polygon can be reclassified with its cells cut into smaller ones.

Of the fifty-two shapes below tested this way, thirty-seven collapse: with smaller cells they tile isohedrally, nearly always by half-turns. Among them are the ten-orbit tetrakis chevron, the six-orbit 7-cell tetrakis shape, and most of the polyrhombilles and polypons. Five more drop to a smaller number of orbits, still above one. Ten keep their isohedral number (five polykites, three polyrhombilles, two polypons), seven of them through two successive refinements. Each card says which. The survivors are the robust hard tiles; the others were hard only because their grid made them so. The fifteen without a note are polyhexes, which need no test, shapes on grids with no refinement available here (squares, triangles, florets), or cases the search did not finish.

They are isolated

One might hope to find such tiles by local search: start from a known hard tile and move one cell. It does not work. Of 816 shapes one cell away from the 28 hardest tiles known at the time of the experiment, 580 do not tile, 220 are isohedral, and none needs more than three orbits. Every shape above was found by classifying an entire size and probing whatever the sweep could not settle.

Reflections are load-bearing

Forbidding reflected copies can only remove tilings, so a one-sided isohedral number is never smaller than the free one. In practice it is rarely larger either. Of the thirteen free polyominoes and polyhexes with isohedral number five or six, eleven do not tile at all without reflections and two keep their number; none gains an orbit. The nine-orbit 11-hex and both eight-orbit 10-hexes are one-sided non-tilers too. When a hard tile uses reflections, it needs them.

Non-tilers and Heesch numbers

A non-tiler's Heesch number is the number of complete rings of copies that can be placed around it before the arrangement must jam. Most non-tilers fail at once; the sweeps dispose of those with a depth-three search. 485 shapes survived that and needed a dedicated proof by SAT solver.

The non-tiling proofs search only placements that could occur in a tiling, which is sound for proving non-tiling but gives only a lower bound on the Heesch number: a finite surround may use a placement that no tiling could. The values below were therefore recomputed over all placements, deepening until a ring provably cannot be completed. For 63 of the 485 shapes the true value is higher than the depth at which non-tiling was first proved.

The star

The non-tiler that took the most effort is a ten-rhombus star. It has no aligned tiling with eighteen or fewer orbits, and backtracking search could not decide whether it could be surrounded even three times. Encoded as a satisfiability problem, the question is settled in seconds: a third ring exists, a fourth does not. The refutation was produced as a DRAT proof and verified with an independent checker, in both the restricted and the unrestricted search space, so its Heesch number is exactly three. (As a 20-iamond, free of the rhombille grid, the same polygon tiles by translation.)
