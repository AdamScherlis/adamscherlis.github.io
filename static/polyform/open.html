Open problems

What the tables do not answer, roughly in the order we would attack them. The working plan, with costs and stopping rules, is in docs/ROADMAP.md in the repository.

  1. What do grid-aligned numbers say about the polygon? On every grid except the hexagonal one, requiring tilings to be aligned can raise a shape's isohedral number, or turn a tiler into a non-tiler. The ten-orbit tetrakis chevron tiles by half-turns as a polyabolo, and so does the six-orbit 7-cell tetrakis shape; the Heesch-3 star tiles by translation as a polyiamond. The grids form refinement chains — a rhombus is two pons or two triangles, a kite is two drafters, a tetrakis triangle is two smaller ones at 45° — so each hard shape can be reclassified on the finest grid that contains it. Which of the sixty-seven six-orbit-or-more shapes keep their number there? A related question is whether alignment can be made a property of the shape itself, by notching its edges, for tilings that use no reflections.
  2. Is there a polyform with isohedral number seven? None of the 81 682 298 shapes here has one, although sixty-two need six orbits and three need eight. Myers's tables show the same gap over a far larger range: no polyomino up to 25 cells, polyhex up to 22, polyiamond up to 30 or polykite up to 25 has isohedral number seven, while eights, nines and tens all occur. Two explanations were tested here and refuted: the six-orbit shapes do not also admit seven-orbit tilings that the minimum hides (none of 26 tested does), and the counting constraints that link n, k and the grid permit seven as freely as six at many sizes. Seven is not forbidden by anything we know.
  3. Why sixes on some grids and fives on others? Grids with several cells per translation unit produce six-orbit tiles in bulk and four-orbit ones at particular parities, and have never produced a five. All thirteen five-orbit shapes are polyominoes, polyhexes or polyiamonds. Of the tilings found for the sixty-seven shapes with six or more orbits, 53 have translations as their only symmetries, typically six tiles per period and nothing more. That suggests a mechanism: a tile forced to use every rotated orientation while permitting no rotation as a symmetry needs as many orbits as the grid has rotations. If that is the rule, the spectrum is explained by the grids' rotation groups and the anomalies — the fives, eights, nines and tens — are the interesting part.
  4. Finding hard tiles without classifying everything. Every hard tile here was found by exhaustive enumeration, and enumeration is running out: the share of anisohedral shapes falls with size on every grid, a row of three million 13-hexes produced nothing above five orbits, and hard tiles have no hard neighbours to climb towards. The alternative is to enumerate tilings instead of shapes: fix a symmetry group and a number of orbits, and grow the tile in all of its positions at once, so that only shapes that tile are ever generated. On the grids where the hard tiles are, very few shapes tile at the frontier — 19 of the 5 376 764 17-rhombilles — so this could reach sizes far beyond any sweep.
  5. Heesch numbers on the new grids. The largest Heesch number known for any polygon is six. The largest found here is four. Exact values are known only for the few hundred non-tilers that needed a special proof; the millions disposed of by the sweeps were only shown to have small ones. A census by family and size, looking for four and above, has not been done on any of the new grids.
  6. Are there aperiodic monotiles on other grids? The method finds the hat and the turtle unprompted and finds nothing else, on any grid, up to the sizes computed. That is a negative result worth extending, and also a reason to look somewhere new: grids whose cells are themselves awkward tiles, taken from a many-orbit tiling of a hard shape, are natural candidates.
  7. Marked tiles. Among edge rules that can be carved into a tile's outline, the highest isohedral number found is three. For the hexagon, the square and the small polyominoes surveyed, every marking without a periodic tiling was proved to have no tiling at all. An edge-marked polyform that tiles only aperiodically would be a different kind of monotile, and nothing here rules one out beyond those prototiles.

Tried, and not worth repeating

  • Bigger sweeps for higher isohedral numbers. Polyhex maxima run 8, 9, 5, 5 at n = 10 to 13; high values are isolated accidents of particular sizes, not a trend.
  • Local search around known hard tiles. Of 816 one-cell neighbours, none exceeds three orbits.
  • Richer mark alphabets on a single hexagon. More labels multiply the examples at each isohedral number without raising the maximum.
  • SAT patches to prove plain shapes non-tilers. Sixty to a hundred times slower than surround search, and proves nothing: a bounded patch can almost always be covered.
  • Compiling the class-based search. No faster than the exact-cover engine once both are compiled.
