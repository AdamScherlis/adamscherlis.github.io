Open problems

What the tables do not answer, roughly in the order we would attack them. The working plan, with costs and stopping rules, is in docs/ROADMAP.md in the repository.

  1. Which grids' conventions are harmless, and why? Requiring tilings to follow a shape's own grid can raise its isohedral number or make a tiler look like a non-tiler. Tested by refinement, the effect varies sharply by family: every anisohedral polykite keeps its number, while most tetrakis shapes and polydrafters, about half the polyrhombilles and polypons, and most of the hard non-tilers on those grids dissolve. For polyhexes there is a one-line proof that tilings must follow the grid. Is there one for polykites? And for the shapes that survive on the other grids, does a third refinement change anything, or can alignment be forced by notching the edges?
  2. Is there a polyform with isohedral number seven? None of the 115 025 990 shapes here has one, although sixty-seven need six orbits and three need eight. Myers's tables show the same gap over a far larger range: no polyomino up to 25 cells, polyhex up to 22, polyiamond up to 30 or polykite up to 25 has isohedral number seven, while eights, nines and tens all occur. Two explanations were tested here and refuted: the six-orbit shapes do not also admit seven-orbit tilings that the minimum hides (none of 26 tested does), and the counting constraints that link n, k and the grid permit seven as freely as six at many sizes. Seven is not forbidden by anything we know.
  3. Why sixes on some grids and fives on others? Grids with several cells per translation unit produce six-orbit tiles in bulk and four-orbit ones at particular parities, and have never produced a five. All thirteen five-orbit shapes are polyominoes, polyhexes or polyiamonds. The stored tilings show what is going on underneath: the commonest hard tiling by far has six tiles to a period, giving three orbits when a half-turn pairs them up and six when nothing does. Counting cells, the number of tiles in a period times n must be divisible by the number of cells c in a translation unit of the grid, so on a grid with c > 1 a period of five or seven tiles is impossible at any size coprime to c. That accounts for the multi-cell grids. It says nothing about polyominoes and polyhexes, where c = 1 and periods of five and ten tiles do occur while seven and fourteen never do.
  4. Finding hard tiles without classifying everything. Every hard tile here was found by exhaustive enumeration, and enumeration is running out: the share of anisohedral shapes falls with size on every grid, a row of three million 13-hexes produced nothing above five orbits, and hard tiles have no hard neighbours to climb towards. Enumerating tilings instead of shapes does not obviously help either: a tiling with six unrelated tiles per period has more ways to arrange its tiles than there are shapes at any size within reach, and specifying a tiler by half its boundary gains only a modest factor. What the stored tilings do offer is a template. A hard tiling is a handful of tiles per period with almost no symmetry, which is specific enough to build candidates by hand rather than wait for a sweep to stumble on them.
  5. Heesch numbers on the new grids. The largest Heesch number known for any polygon is six. The largest found here is four. Exact values are known only for the few hundred non-tilers that needed a special proof; the millions disposed of by the sweeps were only shown to have small ones. A census by family and size, looking for four and above, has not been done on any of the new grids.
  6. Is there another aperiodic monotile? The sweeps find the hat and the turtle unprompted and find nothing else, on any grid, up to the sizes computed. The monotile search looks where the Spectre was found instead: at the point of a family of tiles where its edge lengths coincide and the tile leaves its grid. Over the polygons covered so far the hat's family is still alone. Equilateral polygons that come from no polyform, and families with three edge lengths, have not been searched by anyone.
  7. Marked tiles. Among edge rules that can be carved into a tile's outline, the highest isohedral number found is three. For the hexagon, the square and the small polyominoes surveyed, every marking without a periodic tiling was proved to have no tiling at all. An edge-marked polyform that tiles only aperiodically would be a different kind of monotile, and nothing here rules one out beyond those prototiles.

Tried, and not worth repeating

  • Bigger sweeps for higher isohedral numbers. Polyhex maxima run 8, 9, 5, 5 at n = 10 to 13; high values are isolated accidents of particular sizes, not a trend.
  • Local search around known hard tiles. Of 816 one-cell neighbours, none exceeds three orbits.
  • Richer mark alphabets on a single hexagon. More labels multiply the examples at each isohedral number without raising the maximum.
  • SAT patches to prove plain shapes non-tilers. Sixty to a hundred times slower than surround search, and proves nothing: a bounded patch can almost always be covered.
  • Compiling the class-based search. No faster than the exact-cover engine once both are compiled.
